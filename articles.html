Article 01 Chain Selection Guide

The Engineering Guide: How to Choose the Right Industrial Chain for High-Stakes Applications

In heavy industries like sugar processing, palm oil milling, cement production, and steel manufacturing, mechanical chains are the literal backbone of production. When a chain operates under continuous load, unexpected downtime does not just slow things down — it stops revenue entirely.

Choosing the right industrial chain is a balancing act between tensile strength, wear resistance, environmental tolerance, and long-term cost efficiency. Standard options rarely suffice when facing high abrasive dust, corrosive materials, or extreme shock loads.

Here is an engineering blueprint on how to select the optimal industrial chain for your specific sector's toughest applications.

1. The Core Variables of Chain Selection

Before focusing on industry-specific demands, every procurement manager and plant engineer must evaluate four core parameters:

  • The Nature of the Load: Is it steady transmission, or are there frequent start-stop shocks and heavy structural impacts?
  • Environmental Exposure: Will the chain face highly abrasive particulates (like cement clinker or slag), chemical corrosives (such as palm oil fatty acids), or extreme temperatures (such as steel mill cooling beds and reheat furnaces)?
  • Operating Speed and Geometry: High-speed drive applications require precision roller chains, whereas low-speed, high-mass material transport calls for engineered conveyor chains or forged chains.
  • Lubrication Accessibility: Can the chain be manually lubricated consistently, or does the application require specialised or self-lubricating configurations to prevent premature wear?

2. Industry-Specific Deep Dives

The Sugar Industry: Combating Moisture and Corrosive Acids

Sugar cane processing is notorious for its harsh, highly seasonal operational periods. Chains used in bagasse conveyors, cane harvesters, and main mill drives must endure intense moisture and corrosive organic acids without seizing.

  • The Right Choice: Heavy-duty, high-tensile Cane Carrier and Bagasse Conveyor Chains engineered with optimised pin and bushing clearances.
  • Material Spotlight: Heat-treated alloy steels or premium series like HS Chain and IWIS, which utilise precise metallurgical engineering to ensure pins and bushes resist both the abrasive nature of cane fibres and chemical corrosion.

The Palm Oil Industry: Resisting Shock Loads and Sticky Acids

From fresh fruit bunch (FFB) scraper conveyors to sterilisation cages and kernel extraction units, palm oil mills experience high shock loads alongside constant exposure to acidic fruit juices and steam.

  • The Right Choice: Specialised Palm Oil Conveyor Chains featuring solid rollers and extended pin options.
  • Material Spotlight: High-grade stainless steel components or specialised plating to prevent premature elongation caused by acidic wear. Ensuring the chain is food-grade or built to resist sticky oil buildup keeps maintenance cycles manageable.

The Cement Industry: Winning the Battle Against Abrasive Dust

Cement plants present one of the most punishing environments on Earth for rotating equipment. Bucket elevators, clinker deep-bucket conveyors, and reclaimer systems are continuously bombarded by highly abrasive limestone and clinker dust.

  • The Right Choice: Hardened, heavy-duty Bucket Elevator Chains and Cranked Link Bushed Chains (such as the TRIO Forged Chain series at P.102mm pitch).
  • Engineering Focus: For these environments, look for chains featuring high surface-hardened pins and bushings (carburized or induction-hardened) to resist abrasive wear, along with optimised seal configurations to lock out destructive dust particles.

The Steel Industry: Surviving Extreme Heat and Massive Masses

Steel manufacturing requires moving raw materials, hot slabs, and finished coils under immense weight and extreme radiant heat.

  • The Right Choice: High-capacity Forged Scrap Conveyor Chains and high-precision, heavy-duty Double-Strand Roller Chains.
  • Engineering Focus: Chains operating near blast furnaces or cooling beds require unique heat-treatment processes to prevent the steel from softening under high operating temperatures, ensuring the chain maintains its high breaking load and structural integrity.

3. Premium vs. Cost-Effective Chains: Finding the Right Balance

Maximising your Return on Investment (ROI) means matching the chain's specification to the exact criticality and operating conditions of the machine.

  • When to Choose Premium, High-Specification Brands: For critical, high-speed drives or processes where absolute synchronisation is non-negotiable, investing in premium engineering is essential. Brands like IWIS Precision Roller Chains (Germany) are built to withstand extreme fatigue limits. They drastically reduce early elongation (stretching), minimising the need for constant manual tensioning adjustments and protecting your secondary machinery from expensive breakdowns.
  • When to Choose Cost-Effective, Heavy-Duty Brands: For massive bulk material handling networks, long conveyor paths, or high-wear environments where chains are replaced on a predictable maintenance schedule due to raw material abrasion, cost-effective alternatives offer the best return. Brands like HS Chain deliver rugged, heavily reinforced industrial conveyor chains built specifically to handle abrasive raw materials. For standard drives, UNO Roller Chains offer heavy-duty, ISO-certified reliability, while TRIO Forged Chains provide the massive load capacity required for heavy raw material transport without over-inflating your procurement budget.
Selecting the right industrial chain is more than matching a part number — it is about optimising your entire conveyor or drive system to lower your overall operating costs and reduce unplanned downtime.

Article 02 Chain Types

Types of Chain

There are two types of chain in common use for transmitting power in industrial applications:

  • The roller chain
  • The inverted tooth chain (also called the silent chain)

The Roller Chain

The inner plates are held together by steel bushes, through which pass the pins riveted to the outer links. A roller surrounds each bush and the teeth of the sprockets bear on the roller. The rollers turn freely on the bushes and the bushes turn freely on the pins. All the contact surfaces are hardened so as to resist wear and are lubricated to reduce friction.

A simple roller chain consists of one strand only, but duplex and triplex chains — consisting of two or three strands — may be built up, with each pin passing right through the bushes in the two or three strands simultaneously. Multi-strand configurations increase load capacity without increasing pitch size.

The Inverted Tooth (Silent) Chain

The silent chain is built up from a series of flat plates, each of which has two projections or teeth. The outer faces of the teeth are ground to give an included angle of 60° or, in some cases, 75°, and they bear against the working faces of the sprocket teeth. The inner faces of the link teeth take no part in the drive and are shaped to clear the sprocket teeth.

The required width of chain is built up from a number of these plates arranged alternately and connected together by hardened steel pins which pass through hardened steel bushes inserted in the ends of the links. The pins are riveted over the outside plates.

The chain may be prevented from sliding axially across the face of the sprocket teeth by outside guide plates without teeth, or by a centre guide plate that fits into a recess turned in the sprocket.

How the Silent Chain Engages the Sprocket

When the chain is new, each link, as it enters the sprocket, pivots about the pin on the adjacent link which is in contact with the sprocket. The working faces of the link are thus brought gradually into contact with the corresponding faces of the sprocket teeth — and a similar action takes place as each link leaves the sprocket. Therefore there is no relative sliding between the faces of the links and the faces of the sprocket teeth.

As wear takes place on the pins and bushes, the smooth action of the chain is not impaired, but the chain rides higher up the sprocket teeth and the effective pitch circle diameter of the sprocket increases.

Key Advantage of Silent Chains Unlike roller chains which impact the sprocket at each engagement, the silent chain's gradual meshing action eliminates sliding contact between link faces and sprocket teeth — resulting in significantly quieter operation and reduced shock at high speeds.
Article 03 Chain Construction

Construction of Chain

A chain consists of two rows of inner and outer plates. The outer row of plates is known as the pin link or coupling-link, whereas the inner row of plates is called the roller link. Pins are fitted in the pin link and pass through a bush which is press-fitted into the rollers, joining the assembly together.

The chain rollers are mounted on the bushings and roll over the sprocket teeth during motion. The pins are free inside the bush, allowing the chain to articulate freely at each joint without binding — this is what enables smooth engagement with the sprocket.

Load-Transmitting Capacity

The load-transmitting capacity of a chain drive depends on the pitch size and the sprocket tooth count. Larger pitch chains and larger sprockets increase load capacity. However, to avoid excessively large sprockets in high-torque applications, chains are commonly configured in double-strand and triple-strand widths — each pin passing through the bushes of multiple parallel strands simultaneously.

Each roller link consists of two inner plates, two bushes, and two rollers — every component hardened and precisely dimensioned to ensure consistent engagement and long service life in continuous industrial operation.

Why This Construction Matters for Heavy Industry

The press-fit of the bush into the inner plate creates a tightly constrained pivot bearing at each joint. This design allows the chain to articulate smoothly around the sprocket while maintaining high tensile strength and resisting the rotational forces transmitted by the drive.

The free-rolling roller reduces the contact stress on the sprocket tooth face, distributing load over a larger contact area and significantly extending both chain and sprocket service life — which is particularly important in high-abrasion environments like cement and sugar plants.

Article 04 Technical Reference

Chains — Drive Mechanics, Leaf Chains & Selection Guidance

Through the industrial ages, chains have provided a positive means of transferring power from one shaft to another parallel shaft. Today, bushed roller chains provide a simple, robust drive with the minimum of friction, their manufacture being laid down in BS 228:1984 (ISO 60–1982).

A noted disadvantage of the chain drive is that the centre distance for sprockets must be either adjustable or calculated based on standard chain pitches. Alternatively, a jockey wheel may be used on the slack side — though this generally precludes the drive from being reversed.

Lubrication & Environment Chains must be adequately lubricated and work in a clean environment. Dust and dirt will rapidly accelerate wear, causing the chain to become noisy and elongate prematurely. Sealed environments and automatic lubrication systems significantly extend service intervals in industrial settings.

Leaf Chains

Leaf chains act as balancers between a moveable part and counterweight — or where low-speed pulling is required (tension linkage). This type of chain is also called a balance chain. Two types are commonly provided: AL type and BL type, designated by the number of plates, thickness of plates, and pitch.

Key Properties of Leaf Chains

  • In roller chains, all link plates have higher fatigue resistance due to the compressive stress of press fits. In leaf chains, only the two outer plates are press-fit — therefore the tensile strength of leaf chains is high, but the maximum allowable tension is lower.
  • The more plates used in the lacing, the higher the tensile strength.
  • Pins articulate directly on the plates with very high bearing pressure — regular lubrication with SAE 30 or SAE 40 machine oil is essential.
  • When chain speed exceeds 30 m/min, or if the chain is cycled more than 1,000 times per day, wear accelerates dramatically even with lubrication — RS roller chains should be used instead.
  • AL-type should only be used where there are no shock loads, wear is not a significant concern, and cycles are fewer than 100 per day. Otherwise, BL-type is recommended.

Article 05 Chain Maintenance

When to Replace the Chain

The effect of wear on a roller chain is to increase the spacing of the links, causing the chain to grow longer. Importantly, this is not from any actual stretching of the metal — as too many engineers and mechanics intuitively believe — but is due entirely to the effect of wear at the pivoting parts (pin-bushing contact surfaces). It could more accurately be said that the roller chain loosens with wear rather than stretches.

After a long period of running time, the pitch of the chain increases uniformly, resulting in an overall increase in chain length. As the pitch grows, the chain no longer seats correctly at the base of the sprocket teeth — instead it rides higher and higher up the tooth flanks, accelerating wear on both chain and sprocket.

The Replacement Rule of Thumb

It is advisable to monitor the exact length of a drive chain regularly. The generally accepted industry rule is:

Replace a roller chain that has elongated 3% on an adjustable-centre drive, or 1.5% on a fixed-centre drive.

When the chain length increases to such an extent that the chain can be raised to half the height of the sprocket teeth — i.e. when the meshing is reduced to half — the chain must be replaced immediately to prevent catastrophic failure during operation.

How to Measure Chain Elongation

The most accurate method is to count a known number of pitches (typically 12 or 24 links) on an unstressed, clean chain and measure the total length with a steel ruler or vernier caliper. Compare this to the theoretical new-chain length for the same number of links. The percentage difference is the elongation.

For example: a 24-link section of a ½" pitch chain has a new length of 12 inches. If it now measures 12.36 inches, that is a 3% elongation — time to replace on an adjustable-centre drive.

Practical Maintenance Tip Always replace the chain and sprocket together where possible. A worn sprocket has a tooth profile adapted to a worn chain. Installing a new chain on a worn sprocket will accelerate wear on the new chain almost immediately, negating the value of the replacement and shortening service life significantly.

Article 06 Conveyor Chains

How Do Conveyor Chains Work?

Conveyor chains are an essential component of materials-handling industries — the unsung heroes of industrial operations, facilitating smooth and efficient material flow across a vast range of applications. Understanding the mechanics behind conveyor chains is fundamental for any engineer or plant manager selecting or maintaining a conveyor system.

Understanding Conveyor Chains

In simple terms, a conveyor chain is a series of interconnected links forming a continuous loop. Each link has two key components: the inner link and the outer link. The inner links connect the chain via pins or shafts, while the outer links typically feature plates or rollers that support materials during transportation.

The extreme versatility of conveyor chains has seen them become essential for handling materials as diverse as grain, timber, cement, sugar cane, palm fruit bunches, asphalt, and bulk minerals.

How Conveyor Chains Move Materials

Sprockets or gears propel the chains along a defined path. As chain and sprocket work together, materials placed on or in the conveyor system are transported from one point to another. Different chain and sprocket configurations suit different applications — the combination determines speed, load capacity, and material compatibility.

Types of Conveyor Chains

Roller Chains

The most common type of industrial chain, featuring cylindrical rollers that engage with sprocket teeth. Roller chains are used in conveyor systems for the smooth transportation of materials across a wide range of industrial environments. The first patent for a roller chain was applied in 1800 for canal boat transport.

Hollow Pin Chains

These chains feature hollow pins allowing the insertion of cross rods or other attachments. This makes hollow pin chains a versatile option for handling irregular or fragile materials — widely used in food processing, packaging, and palm oil processing industries where cross-bar attachments are required to carry product.

Double Pitch Chains

Compared to standard roller chains, double pitch chains have elongated pitch lengths providing greater clearance between links. This makes them ideal for transporting larger or bulky items, and for conveyor applications where load is carried between the links rather than on the rollers.

Sharp Top Chains

Sharp top chains feature serrated areas on the top of the links, creating a gripping surface that transports materials with greater stability. Widely used in timber processing industry where cut lumber must be conveyed without slipping.

Article 07 Chain Standards

Understanding Conveyor Chain Standards: British vs. American

There is a huge variety of conveyor chains available, each one suitable for different applications and industries. Yet one of the most important — and often overlooked — factors when selecting the right chain is the difference between British and American conveyor chain standards. Selecting the wrong standard of product can lead to compatibility issues, accelerated wear, and even system breakdowns resulting in costly unplanned downtime.

Why Do Different Standards Matter?

Conveyor chain standards define a wide range of critical factors: dimensions, pitch, pin diameter, roller diameter, and minimum tensile strength. These parameters ensure that chains, sprockets, and related components function reliably within a system regardless of where they are sourced. Mismatching standards — even by a small dimensional difference — will result in poor sprocket engagement, rapid wear, and premature failure.

British Standard Chains (BS / ISO)

British Standard chains, designated under BS or ISO standards (such as BS 4116 or ISO 1977), are widely used in the UK, Europe, Asia, Africa, and throughout Southeast Asia. Compared to their American counterparts, British standard chains are typically manufactured with a larger pitch and feature solid bush construction for increased strength and wear resistance.

Key Features of British Standard Conveyor Chains

  • Pitch sizes defined in metric units
  • Solid bushes for superior lubrication retention and longer service life
  • Commonly used in cement plants, mining operations, timber processing, and bulk material handling
  • Designed to suit British Standard and ISO sprockets for a precise fit
  • Predominantly used throughout Southeast Asia's heavy industrial sector

American Standard Chains (ANSI / ASME)

American Standard chains, designated as ANSI or ASME (such as ANSI B29.1), are more commonly used in North America and in industries and equipment built to US specifications.

Key Features of American Standard Chains

  • Pitch and dimensions follow imperial measurements
  • Typically lighter in construction compared to British standard chains
  • Widely used in sawmills, timber processing, food production, and recycling operations
  • Compatible with ANSI-standard sprockets only — cannot be mixed with BS/ISO sprockets

Choosing the Right Standard

The right standard for your requirements depends on several factors:

  • Your existing equipment: Always check the OEM manufacturer's specifications. The chain standard must match the sprocket.
  • Regional preferences: In Southeast Asia, BS/ISO standard chains dominate cement, mining, palm oil, and sugar applications. ANSI chains are common in general industrial and food-processing equipment.
  • Sprocket availability: Mixing standards is not possible — ensure your entire drive system is specified to the same standard before sourcing.
  • Application demands: High-abrasion and heavy-load applications often benefit from the solid bush construction of BS/ISO chains.
